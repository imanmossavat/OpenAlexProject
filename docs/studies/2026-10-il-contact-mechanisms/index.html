Crawler vs search on a mechanism question: failure causes in imitation-learned contact-rich manipulation

The test question asks which failure mechanisms of imitation-learned robot policies on contact-rich tasks have been shown by intervention, how large the effects are, and whether they replicate. The real question is about the tools. How well do a citation crawler (ArticleCrawler), ChatGPT deep research (two prompts), NotebookLM deep research and a short Claude web search gather that evidence, and how accurate is what they report? Unlike the first field test (NCA), the checklist was written and timestamped before any results were seen, and every number was checked against the paper text.

Authorship and provenance. This study was carried out and written by Claude (Opus 5.5), an AI agent from Anthropic, in a Claude Code session, for Iman Mossavat. Claude is not an author of the ArticleCrawler repository. It used the repository as a tool. Each figure and table carries a label saying who produced it.

Bottom line

  • No single tool found much of the evidence, and the tools barely overlap. The four search outputs cite 3 to 14 of the 36 verified evidence papers each. Only two papers appear in more than one of them. ChatGPT's two runs share no paper with each other or with NotebookLM.
  • Every search output contained factual errors. Against the paper text, ChatGPT with the structured prompt made 3 errors in 9 rows. ChatGPT with the short prompt made 3 errors in 5 rows (two citations could not be found). NotebookLM made 4 errors plus 5 partly-wrong claims. The worst: NotebookLM cites Much Ado About Noising as support for the explanation that paper argues against. Claude's short search had 0 errors in the 5 claims checked.
  • The crawler cannot see half of this field. 17 of the 36 evidence papers (47%) have neither references nor citations in OpenAlex, 16 of them from 2025–2026. A citation crawl reaches such a paper only if it is a seed. Among the 19 papers that do have links, the crawls found 11 of 15 (crawl A) and 7 of 10 (crawl B) of those that were not seeds.
  • Where links exist, the crawl gathers the whole neighbourhood, and that surfaced the best replication evidence. Crawl B holds every OpenAlex-indexed citer of FACTR (17/17), RDP (48/48), ACP (24/24) and robomimic (66/66). Among them was FoAR, which no search tool named. It turned out to be the clearest independent evidence that adding force naively leaves most of its benefit unused.
  • Prompt structure mattered more than the tool. The same ChatGPT deep research returned single-factor ablation evidence with a structured prompt. With a one-paragraph prompt it returned mostly method-vs-baseline claims, the evidence the question excludes, and answered in Dutch.
  • The blind spot holds on a larger, independent set. Of 217 contact-rich imitation-learning works from 2023 on, 95 (44%) have no links in OpenAlex; in 2026, 66 of 103. The crawls reached 68–71% of the linked ones. In the citation network of these works, 65% are isolated.
  • The mechanism evidence is not in the contact-rich literature. In the topic model, most evidence papers fall in a general "IL theory, data and interventions" cluster that holds only 4 contact-rich-titled works. A search limited to contact-rich titles would miss them.
  • Search plus crawl again worked best. Seeding the crawler from search added 5 evidence papers that the crawler cannot reach on its own, including Past-Token Prediction and the grasp-vs-insertion paper (QDP).

Agentic analysis
  • Collection works. The crawls hold all or nearly all OpenAlex-indexed citers of every linked evidence paper.
  • Identification needs reading. None of FACTR's 17 indexed citers re-tests its curriculum. Most are teleoperation hardware papers and surveys. Among ACP's citers, FoAR (real robot, 20 trials per method) tests force fusion directly. Force added as tokens gives a wiping score of 0.575, concatenated 0.475, and contact-gated 0.875, against 0.500 for vision only. That is a cross-group, cross-policy result on FACTR's question, and no search tool named it.
  • Limits. OpenAlex citer counts are low for arXiv-only papers. The dark evidence (for example Past-Token Prediction, Much Ado About Noising, Unpacking Diffusion Policy) has no indexed citers at all, so a crawl cannot test its replication.
  • The search tools did no better. ChatGPT wrote "None found" in every row. NotebookLM listed later methods that adopt a design, which is not a replication.

Prompt sensitivity

ChatGPT deep research ran twice on the same question: once with the structured prompt, once with the question alone. The two outputs share no paper. The structured prompt produced single-factor ablation rows with paper IDs, table numbers and trial counts, and 6 of its 9 rows check out against the paper, although 3 of those 6 fall outside the contact-rich scope. The short prompt produced method-vs-baseline gains without IDs, two citations that could not be found, and an answer in Dutch. The Dutch suggests account language or memory carried over. One structural point holds regardless: the short prompt never defined "intervention", so the tool reported what most papers headline, which is a method beating baselines.

What can be claimed about the crawler

Supported by this study (one question, one day, OpenAlex as of 29 October 2026):

  1. A citation crawl reaches only papers that have citation links in its source database. Here that excluded 17 of 36 evidence papers, almost all from 2025–2026.
  2. Among linked papers that were not seeds, the crawls reached 70–73% (11/15 and 7/10).
  3. On an independent set of 217 contact-rich IL works, 44% had no links in OpenAlex, and the crawls reached 68% (crawl A) and 71% (crawl B) of the linked ones.
  4. For linked evidence papers, the crawl collected all or nearly all of their indexed citers, which is the material needed to check replication.
  5. The crawl surfaced relevant interventional evidence that none of four search outputs named (FoAR).
  6. With paired title keywords, 97–98% of processed papers were on topic.
  7. Each crawl took under 20 minutes and the whole study stayed within a quarter of one free daily OpenAlex allowance.

Not supported: that the crawler replaces search; that it finds recent work; that it identifies replications without a reader; that its own output answers a mechanism question. As in the NCA study, every finding above came from analysis scripts on top of the crawl data.

Features this points to:

  • A fallback reference source for dark papers (arXiv or Semantic Scholar reference lists).
  • Automatic merging of preprint and venue versions.
  • A per-paper "who cites this" report from crawl data.
  • Importing seeds directly from a search result.

Who works on contact-rich imitation learning

Two populations, both from OpenAlex. The seed citers are the 1,450 works that cite at least one of crawl A's seeds. Most cite Diffusion Policy (1,127) or ACT (663), so they describe the wider imitation-learning community. The field set is narrower: the 217 works from 2023 onward whose titles pair an imitation-learning term with a contact term (force, tactile, insertion, assembly, compliance and similar). The exact query is in scripts/agentic/fetch_all.py.

Authors who cite at least three of the four seeds that have citers (FACTR, robomimic, Diffusion Policy, ACT) include Cewu Lu (18 citing papers; FoAR and RDP come from his group), Hiroshi Ito and Tetsuya Ogata (12 and 10, the only ones in this list who cite FACTR), Yuke Zhu (11), Sergey Levine (10) and Chelsea Finn (10).

Coverage by year: the blind spot on a larger set

The 36 evidence papers are a small sample, so the same test was repeated on the 217-work field set, which was defined independently of the crawls. It shows the same pattern. 95 of 217 works (44%) have neither references nor citations in OpenAlex, and in 2026 the share is 66 of 103 (64%). Of the 122 works with links, crawl A reached 83 (68%) and crawl B 87 (71%).

Topics in the literature

The corpus is 1,622 works: the seed citers, the field set and the evidence papers, de-duplicated by title. 1,197 have an abstract in OpenAlex. Titles and abstracts were embedded with SPECTER, reduced with UMAP to 8 dimensions and clustered with k-means. The silhouette score was best at k=6 (0.389). k=11 (0.363) was used because it separates tactile policies, assembly, surgical robotics and teleoperation hardware, which k=6 merges. Keywords come from class-based TF-IDF. The topic names are Claude's, from the keywords and the papers nearest each cluster centre. The method is described in docs/studies/agentic-analyses.md.

How to read it. The field set (contact-rich titles) sits mostly in three topics: assembly and insertion (64), tactile and force-aware policies (46), and contact-rich bimanual compliant policies (36). The mechanism evidence sits elsewhere. Most of it (robomimic, Diffusion Policy, Much Ado About Noising, the Pitfalls paper, MILE, IntervenGen, MinInter) falls in "IL theory, data and interventions", which contains only 4 field-set works. In other words, the papers that establish mechanisms are general imitation-learning papers, not contact-rich ones. A search restricted to contact-rich titles would miss them.

Agentic analysis

Crawl B reached 7 field-set works that crawl A did not, and crawl A none that B missed. Two of the seven were search seeds (QDP, ContactDP); the others came through links, for example VIDP, rebar-cage assembly and DMP-based contact-rich teleoperation. Most of crawl B's extra papers entered as references of older learning-from-demonstration surveys: Learning and generalization of motor skills by learning from demonstration (2009) brought in 484, Robot Learning from Demonstration (2012) 461, and Robot Learning from Demonstration in Robotic Assembly: A Survey (2018) 227. That is volume, not relevance, and it matches the NCA finding that more papers does not mean more of the topic.
