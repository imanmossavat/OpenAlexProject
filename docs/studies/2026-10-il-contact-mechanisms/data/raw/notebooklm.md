Empirical Analysis of Interventional Failure Mechanisms in Imitation-Learned Contact-Rich Robot ManipulationIn imitation-learned robotic manipulation, determining why policies fail during contact-rich tasks—such as peg-in-hole insertion, wiping, block pushing, and multi-part assembly—requires rigorous interventional evaluation. Observational correlation often conflates peripheral anomalies with true root causes. Controlled interventional studies isolate causal mechanisms by modifying isolated system variables—such as loss functions, action representations, sensing modalities, temporal prediction horizons, or trajectory recomposition strategies—while holding all other environment and training conditions constant.This report synthesizes interventional evidence across robot learning paradigms, detailing six core failure mechanisms verified by interventional ablations on physical hardware and high-fidelity physics simulators.Causal Failure Mechanisms Isolated by Empirical InterventionsMultimodal Action Averaging and Optimization DiscontinuitiesWhen a policy trained with standard continuous regression objectives, such as Mean Squared Error or unimodal Gaussian heads, encounters states with multiple valid expert trajectories, it minimizes loss by outputting the conditional expectation of those actions [cite: 1, 2]. In contact-rich settings where approaching an object from either the left or the right yields zero loss, but hitting it head-on causes a collision, averaging multimodal actions forces the robot into unviable intermediate regions [cite: 1]. Furthermore, hard physical contacts create non-smooth, discontinuous energy landscapes that continuous unimodal policies cannot approximate without unstable high-frequency oscillations [cite: 1, 3, 4].To isolate this failure mode, interventional studies ablated standard continuous regression heads against expressive conditional density estimators, including Energy-Based Implicit Behavior Cloning, Conditional Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models, and Flow Matching or Generalized Policy Imitation formulations [cite: 1, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Evaluated across simulated environments like the Robomimic suite and the Push-T benchmark, as well as physical hardware platforms like Franka Emika Panda and ALOHA bimanual setups, the choice of objective function demonstrated a massive impact on policy viability [cite: 1, 2, 6, 7, 9]. On the physical Push-T task, replacing continuous regression baselines with an end-to-end Diffusion Policy increased task success from 20% for the best GMM variant and 0% for Implicit BC to 95% [cite: 1]. Across the Robomimic simulation benchmark, replacing unimodal heads with Diffusion Policy yielded an average performance improvement of 46.9% across state and vision observations [cite: 2]. On high-precision contact tasks like Tool-Hang, flow-matching and diffusion formulations maintained success rates above 80%, whereas continuous unimodal models routinely failed during initial hook engagement [cite: 6, 8].This causal relationship has been widely replicated across diverse policy architectures and contact regimes. Triply-Hierarchical Diffusion Policy replicated the advantages of multi-scale diffusion representations, showing a +27.5% average relative improvement over standard Diffusion Policy across 44 simulation tasks and +72.4% on real-world bimanual contact tasks [cite: 10]. Generalized Policy Imitation demonstrated that non-parametric flow composition outperforms diffusion heads while reducing inference latency from 67 ms to 3.3 ms on Push-T [cite: 7]. Visuotactile Flow Policy replicated the superiority of flow matching over standard DDIM diffusion on physical match lighting and whiteboard erasing tasks, elevating success from 30% to 80% [cite: 8].Compounding Distribution Shift and Out-of-Distribution Contact DriftStandard open-loop Behavioral Cloning operates under an implicit Markovian assumption during offline training. Small execution errors compound exponentially over extended execution horizons [cite: 11, 12, 13]. In contact-rich regimes, minor trajectory deviations shift the robot end-effector into physical states, such as wedged pegs, surface binding, or unexpected friction, that were never demonstrated in the expert training dataset [cite: 14]. Because the policy lacks expert supervision for these out-of-distribution recovery states, execution degrades catastrophically [cite: 13, 14].Interventional ablations isolated this mechanism by comparing offline human demonstrations against datasets augmented with targeted error-recovery trajectories. These recovery trajectories were generated through human-in-the-loop interventions like DAgger and HG-DAgger [cite: 13, 14, 15], supervisor disturbance noise injection via DART [cite: 13], interventional trajectory recomposition via Interventional MimicGen [cite: 14], and policy-driven exploration generators such as DiscoDemo and MILE [cite: 11, 12, 16]. Evaluations spanned simulated and physical precision insertion tasks, including robosuite Nut-and-Peg Assembly, bimanual peg insertion, LunarLander, and multi-stage assembly platforms [cite: 14, 15, 16].In physical and simulated peg-insertion experiments, augmenting offline datasets with interventional recovery data raised policy success rates from 39%–40% for baseline Behavioral Cloning to 78%–80% [cite: 11, 12, 16]. Interventional MimicGen demonstrated that incorporating a handful of human interventions into automated dataset generation enabled zero-shot mistake recovery during contact failure states, achieving robust task completion without requiring exhaustive full-trajectory teleoperation [cite: 14]. DART ablations confirmed that injecting calibrated Gaussian noise into the expert's control signal during demonstration collection forced the supervisor to demonstrate recovery behaviors, eliminating compounding drift and matching expert performance with significantly higher data efficiency [cite: 13].Replication across algorithmic frameworks has been extensive. Predicting relative displacement or velocity commands causes integrated spatial drift over time [cite: 1, 17]. Small errors in velocity predictions accumulate, leading to growing positional offsets [cite: 1, 17]. In high-precision contact tasks, even millimeter-scale cumulative drift leads to surface collisions or missed insertion clearances [cite: 1, 18]. Conversely, absolute position commands tie actions directly to global or anchor-relative spatial frames, preventing cumulative spatial drift [cite: 1, 17, 19, 20].Controlled experiments isolated this parameterization by holding all network architecture choices constant while altering only the action output head between relative pose updates and absolute end-effector poses [cite: 1, 17, 21]. Evaluated on Push-T, Robomimic Square, MimicGen 12-task manipulation suites, and physical robot setups, absolute position control consistently outperformed velocity control across all vision- and state-based manipulation benchmarks [cite: 1, 2, 17, 21]. Absolute position-control Diffusion Policy maintained peak performance under up to four execution steps of simulated control latency, whereas velocity-control policies degraded rapidly when execution lagged behind observation [cite: 1, 17]. Equivariant Diffusion Policy ablations confirmed that parameterizing actions in absolute SE(3) pose vectors, combined with $SO(2)$ symmetry constraints, improved task success by an average of 21.9% across 12 MimicGen tasks compared to standard non-equivariant baselines [cite: 21].This causal relationship has been confirmed across several distinct policy frameworks. 3D Diffusion Policy and Spatially Conditioned Diffusion Policy adopted absolute spatial position and pose representations to eliminate drift, achieving greater than 80% success across Meta-World and DexArt benchmarks [cite: 20, 22]. Optimizing the action chunk horizon $H$ and the prediction horizon $k$ establishes a critical balance between temporal consistency and closed-loop reactivity [cite: 17, 24].Interventional studies varied the prediction horizon $H$, action chunking size $k$, and temporal ensembling strategies, comparing receding-horizon execution against non-overlapping chunk execution [cite: 1, 17, 24]. A primary interaction occurs between sensory input limitations and temporal chunking parameters. When visual perception is degraded by self-occlusion during close-range contact, a vision-only policy effectively operates open-loop [cite: 8, 18, 28]. Under these conditions, predicting long action horizons without real-time contact feedback forces the policy to commit to spatial commands that may be slightly misaligned [cite: 17, 23, 25]. This open-loop commitment amplifies compounding spatial drift, driving the robot end-effector into out-of-distribution contact failure states [cite: 13, 14]. Integrating real-time tactile or proprioceptive force signals breaks this loop by providing immediate physical feedback, allowing receding-horizon action execution to dynamically adjust to mechanical resistance [cite: 18, 33].A second critical interaction connects action parameterization with dataset distribution shift. Relative velocity representations accumulate spatial tracking errors over long trajectories [cite: 1, 17]. In contrast, absolute position parameterization grounds action predictions in absolute spatial coordinates, which suppresses drift [cite: 1, 17, 19, 20]. However, absolute position commands require accurate local compliance when contacting rigid surfaces [cite: 18, 29, 30]. If the dataset contains non-physical linear interpolations from trajectory recomposition, policies trained with absolute position control will attempt to enforce those non-physical trajectories directly onto rigid boundaries [cite: 34]. This results in excessive contact forces or surface binding [cite: 30, 34]. Consequently, achieving robust performance requires combining absolute spatial action heads with interpolated-data filtering algorithms like MinInter, alongside force-reflective or compliant control layers [cite: 18, 29, 30, 34].Finally, objective function selection interacts directly with dataset collection strategies. Expressive generative objectives such as Diffusion Policy or Flow Matching prevent action averaging in multimodal regions of the expert demonstration space [cite: 1, 4, 7, 8]. However, expressive density models cannot generate correct recovery actions for states that lie entirely outside the support of the training distribution [cite: 1, 13, 14]. Thus, while diffusion and flow matching resolve multimodal action ambiguity, they must be paired with interventional data collection methods—such as DART, DAgger, or I-MG—to provide explicit recovery supervision for out-of-distribution contact states [cite: 13, 14].Nuanced Conclusions and System Design RecommendationsThe empirical findings synthesized in this report yield direct, actionable principles for designing imitation learning pipelines for contact-rich robotic manipulation:Objective Function Formulation: Unimodal regression heads employing Mean Squared Error or simple Gaussian representations should be avoided for contact-rich tasks [cite: 1, 4]. Pipelines should standardize on expressive multimodal representations, such as Conditional Diffusion, Flow Matching, or Energy-Based formulations, to handle non-smooth contact energy landscapes and multimodal trajectory choices [cite: 1, 4, 7, 8].Action Head Architecture: Action output spaces should be parameterised using absolute spatial poses rather than relative velocity updates [cite: 1, 17, 20]. Absolute position parameterization prevents integration drift and maintains policy performance under execution latency [cite: 1, 17].Temporal Horizon and Context Configuration: Action horizons should be tuned to balance temporal consistency and closed-loop reactivity [cite: 17, 24]. Predicting action chunks of moderate length ($H \approx 8 - 16$) combined with receding-horizon execution or temporal ensembling resolves high-frequency jitter while preserving the ability to react to contact switches [cite: 1, 9, 17, 24]. Where history-dependent stages create visual ambiguity, full temporal context via state-space models or history buffers should be incorporated [cite: 3, 26].Multimodal Sensing and Compliant Execution: Visual inputs must be complemented with active tactile sensing, force-torque measurements, or proprioceptive motor-effort signals [cite: 8, 18, 29, 30]. Policies operating in hard physical contact regimes should output force references or utilize compliant admittance layers to prevent mechanical binding and excessive force application [cite: 18, 29, 30, 32].Data Generation and Augmentation Protocols: Offline demonstration datasets should be augmented with explicit error-recovery data collected via interactive intervention or supervisor disturbance injection [cite: 11, 13, 14]. When synthesizing demonstrations via trajectory segment composition, pipelines should incorporate interpolation-minimizing selection methods to prevent non-physical transitions from contaminating the training data [cite: 34].
